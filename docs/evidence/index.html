Evidence · updated 8 October 2026

What is known about range and depth in scientific figures

This page gathers what the literature establishes, what the standards say, and what we have measured, each marked by how strong it is. It covers both halves of the name: the range a screen can show above white, and the depth — the number of levels — that the eye and the data each need. Citations were checked against Crossref and against the papers' own abstracts.

Summary

Established

People see about 3.7 orders of magnitude at once [1]. An 8-bit SDR figure clips much of that; HDR displays can show it.

Established

12-bit PQ is at or below the visibility threshold across 0.001 to 10,000 cd/m² [2]. Nothing beyond 12 bits helps the eye [3].

Measured

A 10-bit PQ step is 1.1 to 1.4 just-noticeable differences (DICOM GSDF); a 12-bit step is about 0.33.

Established

Evidence on more grey levels is mixed. A pilot study found 10-bit better than 8-bit [4]; a three-site study found no accuracy difference, only faster reading [19]. More luminance alone did not help [5]. An HDR prototype display gave small gains on hard cases [6].

Established

What people prefer does not predict what is scientifically useful (R² = 0.31) [7]. Preference alone is not evidence.

Measured

HDR figures display today on a phone and a desktop monitor, as gain-map JPEG, 16-bit PNG, AVIF and HDR video. PDF has no portable HDR yet [8].

Measured

A roll-off that flattens at the top cannot be read back. At 2× headroom it put 31.4 % of a CT's declared range into the last 3 % of brightness. The profile's curve now keeps rising.

Measured

On six real datasets, HDR helped most where the range above white was large (a gravitational wave at 327 × the noise) and added little where it was under 4 × (the M87 black hole).

Open

No study has shown that HDR helps people read scientific figures. Rendering scientific data for HDR displays was explored in 2005, on the prototype displays of the time [23]. We found nothing since for today's consumer HDR screens, and no standard for it.

Vision and bits

How much range and precision the eye can use

  • Range. Adaptation spans about 14 orders of magnitude (starlight to sunlight), but the range seen at once is about 3.7 orders [1]. A single view on a display fits within that.
  • Precision. The DICOM Grayscale Standard Display Function (Barten's model) puts the smallest visible luminance step at about 0.65 % above 100 cd/m², coarser in the dark (2.4 % at 1 cd/m²) [9].
  • Encoding. PQ (SMPTE ST 2084) was designed so that 12-bit steps sit at or below threshold over 0.001–10,000 cd/m² [2]. An evaluation of HDR colour encodings found about 11 bits visually lossless [3].
  • Colour. BT.2020 primaries cover about 76 % of visible chromaticities and almost all real surface colours.

Prior research

Nearby work, and how this differs

  • HDR data on ordinary (SDR) displays: the opposite direction. Visibility-matching [10] and display-adaptive [11] tone mapping compress a scene's range for a given display. Park & Montag compared nine tone-mapping operators on scientific images; the best depended on the data type [7]. Zhou et al. combined tone mapping with simulated glare to flag high values and found the glare preattentive [12]. All of these keep the output within SDR.
  • Colour for science. Colour maps should be perceptually uniform and readable by everyone [13]. Jzazbz is a uniform colour space that extends to HDR [14]; UDeep's HDR colour maps are built in it. Astronomy's asinh stretch shows a wide range in 8 bits [15].
  • Comparing figures. Juxtaposition, superposition and explicit encoding [16]. UDeep's fair HDR/SDR toggle is a juxtaposition that holds the SDR part identical.
  • Measuring visible differences. ColorVideoVDP predicts visible differences with a model of the display and viewer [17].
  • Scientific data on HDR displays: the closest prior work. Ghosh, Trentacoste and Heidrich rendered volume data for HDR displays in 2005, when such displays were research prototypes [23]. HDR VolVis (2006) handled volume data with a high range, for ordinary displays [24]. Vision scientists have built lab HDR luminance displays as instruments [25].

The difference: that early work targeted prototype hardware and one kind of rendering. None of it defines how a scientific figure should carry its range on today's consumer HDR screens, with a declared mapping and an SDR figure of record, and no file profile exists for it. The search so far covered the web, a scholarly full-text index and GitHub (September 2026). It is not yet systematic, so "to our knowledge" is the right wording.

Does it help people?

Observer studies with more range or more bits

Reading: extra precision has helped in one pilot study and made no accuracy difference (but faster reading) in a larger one; extra luminance alone has not; a true HDR display helped a little, mostly in dark regions. None of these studies used scientific figures, so a task-based study is the missing piece. Marchessoux's abstract gives no significance test for its differences.

So CT windowing is the Extend case in daily practice: the data holds two ranges, and the display shows one at a time. An HDR mode could keep the chosen window unchanged and show what lies above it. Diagnostic use of any new display mode is regulated, and would need clinical evidence first.

Comfort and accessibility

What guidance exists for bright content

  • Flashing is limited; steady brightness is not. WCAG 2.3.1 and ITU-R BT.1702 limit flashes to three per second. A review of international guidelines found only two that address HDR, and both concern flashing [26]. We found no numerical limit on static brightness or bright area.
  • The agreed mitigation is user control. The CSS HDR draft says that "some individuals may have a sensitivity to very bright colors, so user agents should provide a mechanism to limit the maximum luminance at user option" [27].
  • Bright areas are assumed to be small. HDR production practice reserves levels above reference white for small highlights.

So the profile forbids flashing above white, requires that switching between HDR and SDR is started by the user, and asks pages to offer a limit. Every page on this site has a "Limit HDR" control. A static HDR figure is not a flash hazard; an animation or video can be.

How to test

Lessons from getting it wrong

  • Put a reference in the image. CT figures at 2× white were judged "not HDR" on a screen that clearly showed HDR for a pattern with a 1× white band beside the brighter squares.
  • Use controls. An SDR control must not glow, and a positive control must glow. Without them, "everything looks smooth" cannot be told apart from "the test cannot show bands".
  • Blind the banding test. Shuffle the strips and keep the key.
  • Verify the stimulus. Decode every file before showing it. One common image library silently reads 16-bit colour PNGs as 8-bit.
  • View locally. Remote-desktop sessions deliver 8-bit SDR.
  • Record conditions: device, OS and browser versions, brightness, and room light.

Open questions

What would settle it

  • A task study: can people find a faint feature, or read a value, faster or more accurately with HDR figures than with SDR?
  • More devices in the evidence table: Android, Windows laptops, OLED TVs, Macs. Send a report.
  • Whether steady brightness above white is uncomfortable, at what level and area. No guideline gives a number.
  • The model comparisons above, repeated with the current curve.
  • Whether 12-bit video plays, and whether decoders can keep 12 bits for stills.
  • Gain-map AVIF, and a CSS media-query SDR fallback inside SVG. Both are untested.
  • A systematic literature search before any claim of novelty.
